Playbook/Coach’s guide
Install the habits before the whole library

A small lineup.
A connected team.

This team already has skill. Give them space to use it, short reads they can recognize, and a defense that turns effort into stops. Size still matters at the rim and on the glass; speed helps most when all five make the same decision.

The offensive identity

Secure the rebound, get a clean outlet, and run wide. If the first advantage closes, flow into five-out. Start with pass, cut, fill; add one screen and one counter after players can maintain spacing. A quick decision can be a pass, a drive, or a controlled reset.

Do not make long threes the price of spacing. Put each player where a catch threatens a useful shot, drive, or pass. Teach every player to screen and handle, including your two bigger boys.

The defensive identity

Man-to-man with support: contain the ball, shrink the nearby gaps, and move weak-side defenders toward a position where they see both ball and their own player. Help is temporary; recover or communicate a specific exchange.

Pressure is useful when it slows decisions without giving up layups. Make the rebound part of every stop. Add full-court pressure after the team can contain and recover in the half court.

These are mastery stages, not fixed weeks. Keep only a few game calls active. The library gives you progressions and answers over the season.

Teach with the animation

  1. Choose a defensive read and show the setup.
  2. Step to the screen plant or defensive commitment and pause.
  3. Ask players to name the defender and the available space.
  4. Play the outcome, then change the defense and ask again.
  5. Rehearse slowly, then let a live defender choose.

X1–X5 stay individually identifiable. Matchup lines show assignments; gold marks a planted screen. Next-movement lines point to the next teaching position, not a guaranteed path through contact.

Measure useful basketball

  • Clean entries / attempted entries.
  • Controlled advances / press possessions.
  • Correct reads, including a good decision to reset.
  • Stops completed with a defensive rebound.
  • Paint touches allowed and avoidable pressure fouls.

Use these to decide the next practice focus. A missed open finish can follow a good read; a made difficult shot can hide a poor one. Do not reward steals at the cost of repeated layups.

High-school principles and your event rules

This playbook uses NFHS-style fundamentals as requested. It does not assume that every Utah youth event uses identical modifications. The public Utah Bantam FAQ does not establish the exact rules. Before using the press, confirm pressing/trapping permissions, any score-margin restrictions, game timing, and the governing rules.

  • Screens: establish a legal stationary position before contact. Keep arms close. Allow an unseen stationary opponent a normal step, and a moving opponent time and distance to avoid the screen. Never teach “wait for contact” as a requirement.
  • Throw-ins: release within five seconds of the ball being at the thrower’s disposal. Use the assigned spot for a designated-spot throw-in. After an opponent’s made basket, permitted endline movement creates a different entry situation.
  • Backcourt: teach the ten-second advance and avoid returning the ball to the backcourt after frontcourt control. A throw-in exception is not permission for a routine live-ball reversal over midcourt.
  • Paint and contact: cutters must keep moving through the lane rather than camping for a feed. Rebounding position is not permission to hold, shove, or undercut.

The animations are deliberately schematic. Playback speed, marker width, and court art are teaching aids, not measurements for legal screen distance or an official’s count.
